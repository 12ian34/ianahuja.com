the black hole

a rotating (kerr) black hole, ray traced from its metric. the spin slider runs from a non-rotating hole out to a* = 0.998, the fastest an accreting hole can realistically get. every distance is in schwarzschild radii.

the mass selector is the interesting one, because it changes nothing you can see. lensing, shadow shape, time dilation and the whole image depend only on r/Rs, so a ten solar mass hole and M87* look pixel for pixel identical. what mass changes is what it would be like to be there:

10 M⊙Rs 30 km · hover at 3 Rs 2 × 1010 g · orbit 4.6 ms
Sgr A*Rs 1.3 × 107 km · hover 49,000 g · orbit 33 min
M87*Rs 1.9 × 1010 km · hover 32 g · orbit 34 days

the physics

schwarzschild metric

the geometry of spacetime around a non-rotating mass. all the visual effects below emerge from this single equation.

schwarzschild radius

the event horizon. nothing that crosses this boundary can escape, including light.

photon geodesic

each pixel traces a light ray through curved spacetime. the binet equation governs the orbital shape, where u = 1/r. the extra u² term (absent in newtonian gravity) is what causes light to spiral and lens.

ray tracing acceleration

the shader integrates this equivalent 3D form at every step. h is the photon's angular momentum, r its distance from the singularity. because h is conserved along a geodesic it is computed once at the camera and reused, which leaves an acceleration that depends only on position. that makes velocity verlet the natural integrator: second-order accurate for the same cost as the first-order euler step it replaces. 300 steps per pixel, adaptive step size.

critical impact parameter

photons with impact parameter below this value are captured by the black hole. photons exactly at this value orbit forever on the photon sphere. this defines the size of the black hole's shadow.

spin: the kerr metric

spin drags spacetime around with it. the horizon shrinks from 1 Rs to 0.53 Rs at the thorne limit, and the innermost stable orbit collapses from 3 Rs all the way to 0.62 Rs, so the disc reaches far closer in and orbits far faster. the shadow stops being a circle: it flattens on the side rotating toward you, because prograde photons can hold on closer than retrograde ones.

none of the schwarzschild shortcuts survive this. angular momentum about the axis is still conserved, but the trick of reducing everything to an acceleration that depends only on position is gone, so the shader integrates the hamiltonian equations directly, in boyer-lindquist coordinates, with runge-kutta.

the carter constant

kerr geodesics have a third conserved quantity beyond energy and angular momentum, which is what makes them integrable at all. it pins the polar motion exactly, and re-projecting onto it after every step is the only thing that keeps the spin axis stable: boyer-lindquist is singular there, and an integrator left to its own devices paints a fan of artefacts straight up it.

zero angular momentum observers

the camera cannot be a static observer any more. inside the ergosphere nothing can hold still against the frame dragging, and for a fast spin the ergosphere swallows the ISCO and everything near it. so the camera is a ZAMO instead: the locally non-rotating frame, carried around with the hole, which stays valid right down to the horizon. at zero spin it is exactly the static observer and nothing changes.

the lapse α is that observer's clock rate against infinity, and it is what the dilation readout shows. it also appears in the ray setup, where the frame dragging term means which way you look changes the photon's energy at infinity.

turning around

past the photon sphere the shadow covers more than half the sky, so pointing the camera at the hole shows nothing at all: the view is entirely black, correctly. the way out is behind you. the camera turns around at that point, and what you see is the whole outside universe crushed into a bright disc, shrinking as you descend, cut in half by the accretion disc seen edge on.

the sky is also violently blueshifted by then, by 1/α, with intensity going as the fourth power of that. near the horizon that is six orders of magnitude, which is both physically correct and completely unreadable, so the exposure adapts past about thirty times. it is the one place the render deliberately departs from the raw radiance, and the colour shift is left alone.

photon sphere

the radius of unstable circular photon orbits. light here can loop the black hole before escaping, creating the sharp bright ring at the shadow's edge.

accretion disk (ISCO)

the innermost stable circular orbit sets the inner edge of the disk. matter inside this radius spirals rapidly inward.

disk temperature

the accretion disk follows the shakura-sunyaev temperature profile, with a zero-torque boundary at the inner edge. the bracketed term forces the temperature to zero at the ISCO, so the disk does not simply get hotter forever as you move inward. it peaks at r = (49/36) rin ≈ 4.1 Rs and falls away on both sides.

turbulence and hot spots

a real disc is not smooth. magnetorotational turbulence stirs it constantly while differential rotation shears every eddy into a trailing spiral, because the inner disc laps the outer one. the shader samples noise in the frame co-rotating with the gas, which produces that shearing for free, and decorrelates it at the local orbital rate so eddies live a fraction of an orbit. without that lifetime the shear accumulates forever and the disc slowly winds itself into infinitely thin stripes.

two brighter spots orbit at 4.4 and 7.1 Rs, standing in for the flares seen from Sgr A*. they are hotter rather than merely brighter, so they run through the same g factor as everything else: the fourth power turns a 40% temperature rise into four times the brightness, and beaming multiplies it again as the spot swings toward you.

orbital velocity

the speed of disk matter as measured by a static observer sitting at that radius. the newtonian √(GM/r) is not enough this close in; the correction in the denominator is what makes matter at the ISCO orbit at exactly half the speed of light.

relativistic doppler shift

disk matter orbiting toward you is blue-shifted, matter receding is red-shifted. the γ factor in the denominator is transverse time dilation: at half the speed of light it red-shifts both sides of the disk, even the material moving exactly sideways.

gravitational redshift

separately from any motion, light climbing out of the gravity well loses energy. at the ISCO this alone costs a photon 18% of its frequency. at the horizon it costs all of it, which is why the shadow is black.

the redshift factor

the two effects above combine into a single number g, the ratio of observed to emitted frequency. everything the disk looks like comes out of this one quantity. at the ISCO, edge-on, it ranges from 1.41 on the approaching side to 0.47 on the receding side: a factor of three in frequency across a single ring.

beaming, from one number

specific intensity divided by frequency cubed is a relativistic invariant, so integrating over the spectrum gives a bolometric brightness that scales as g to the fourth. the temperature shifts by the same factor. put those together with the stefan-boltzmann law and the whole thing collapses: the observed brightness is just the observed temperature to the fourth power. beaming, gravitational dimming and colour shift all fall out of g at once, with nothing hand-tuned.

blackbody colour

the disk colour is not a gradient chosen by eye. each pixel takes its observed temperature, evaluates planck's law along the planckian locus, and converts to linear sRGB. that factor of three in frequency across the ISCO becomes roughly 6500 K on the approaching limb and 2000 K on the receding one: blue-white grading to deep red, entirely from physics.

one honest caveat: a real thin disk around a 6.5 billion solar mass black hole peaks in the far ultraviolet, and a literal render would be a featureless blue-white. the temperature scale here is shifted down into the visible band so the structure is actually legible.

gravitational time dilation

clocks near the black hole tick slower. at 3 Rs, time runs at 82% of the rate far away. at the event horizon, time stops entirely for a distant observer.

the shadow grows as you approach

the shadow is not a fixed object with a fixed size. its angular radius for an observer hovering at r follows from the critical impact parameter, and it grows fast: 3.7° from 40 Rs, 14° at 10, 45° at 3, and exactly 90° at the photon sphere, where the hole swallows precisely half of everything you can see. inside that, more than half the sky is black.

your own frame is stretched

a hovering observer's radial ruler is stretched by 1/√(1 − Rs/r), so the angle you measure between a ray and the straight-down direction is not the coordinate angle the geodesic integrator works in. matching impact parameters converts one to the other. far away it is the identity and nothing changes; close in it is what makes the shadow come out the right size.

the sky blueshifts as you descend

light falling toward you gains energy on the way down, which is the same effect as gravitational redshift read backwards. the entire outside universe shifts blue by the reciprocal of the dilation factor and brightens by its fourth power: at 2 Rs the sky arrives 41% bluer and four times brighter. this is the same g factor as the disc, applied to the background instead.

what it would take to hover

the readout in the corner is the proper acceleration you would need to hold station, which is the newtonian value divided by the dilation factor. it diverges at the horizon, but for a hole this big it stays surprisingly mild for a surprisingly long time: 0.2 g at 40 Rs, about a fifth of standing on earth. 2.5 g at 10 Rs. 33 g at the innermost stable orbit, which would kill you, and 85 g at 2 Rs.

tides are the opposite story. the stretch across a two-metre body at 3 Rs is about 2 × 10−11 m/s2, roughly a trillionth of a g, because tidal force falls as 1/r3 while Rs grows with mass. spaghettification is a small-black-hole problem. here the thrust would crush you long before the tides noticed you.

gravitational lensing angle

the weak-field deflection angle for a photon grazing a mass at distance b. near the black hole the deflection is much larger, and this formula breaks down as rays wrap fully around.

how it's rendered

the background is the real sky

15,598 stars from the HYG catalogue, everything down to magnitude 7, a little past the naked-eye limit. each star's colour is its catalogued B-V index converted to an effective temperature and then run through the same planck curve the disc uses, so sirius arrives at 10,000 K and blue-white, betelgeuse at 3,800 K and red, with nothing chosen by hand.

the catalogue is baked into a cube map rather than an equirectangular image, because the geodesics sweep the entire sphere and equirect would put its seam and its pole singularity exactly where rays are bent hardest. the milky way is layered on procedurally in galactic coordinates, so its band runs where the catalogue's stars are genuinely dense. all of it is lensed: orbit around and you can watch real constellations stretch into arcs as they pass behind the hole.

the lens has to open up

a rectilinear lens, the kind that keeps straight lines straight, cannot show a field much past 120°: the projection runs off to infinity at a right angle. but the shadow passes 90° before you reach the photon sphere, so a fixed narrow lens pointed at the hole goes completely black long before you arrive, which is the least interesting possible view of a black hole.

so the camera uses an equidistant projection, where distance from the centre of the frame is proportional to angle rather than to a tangent, and the field of view opens as you descend to track the shadow. 37° at 40 Rs, 88° at 6, 170° at 3 and closer. the distortion is real and worth knowing about, but it is the honest trade: it is the only way to keep the whole thing in frame.

progressive accumulation

one geodesic per pixel per frame is not enough to resolve the photon ring without aliasing. each frame jitters the sample point within its pixel using a low-discrepancy sequence and blends into a half-float buffer, converging on a 16-sample anti-aliased image whenever the camera settles.

accumulation only works on frames that line up, so how much history is kept is tied to how far the image actually slid: a still camera converges deeply, a slow drift keeps a few frames, and a drag falls back to a single sample and stays responsive.

tone mapping

the disk spans about two and a half orders of magnitude in brightness between its approaching and receding limbs, which no display can show directly. the render is kept in linear light throughout, bloomed above a threshold, then mapped through an ACES filmic curve. the glow around the bright limb is the physical dynamic range, not an effect layered on top.
